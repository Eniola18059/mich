Forces: the pushes and pulls that move our world

What is a force?

A force is a push or a pull on an object. We measure force in newtons (N).

A force can't be seen, but we can see what it does. Forces can make an object do five things:

Examples: a kicked football starts moving, a goalkeeper's glove stops it, a tennis racket changes its direction, and squeezing clay changes its shape.

Contact and non-contact forces

Contact force

The two objects must touch. Applied force, normal force and friction are all contact forces.

Non-contact force

The force works across a gap, with no touching. Magnetism and gravity are non-contact forces.

Applied force

An applied force is a push or pull that a person or object puts on another object by touching it.
  • Bigger applied force means a bigger change in motion.
  • The arrow points the way the force acts, and a longer arrow is a stronger force.
  • Examples: kicking, throwing, lifting, pulling a wagon, opening a drawer.

Normal force

The normal force is the push of a surface back on an object resting on it. It always acts at 90° (perpendicular) to the surface.

Gravity pulls the book down. The table pushes back up with the same amount of force, so the book doesn't fall through. The two forces are balanced.

Equal and opposite arrows: balanced forces.
On a ramp the normal force tilts with the surface, but it is always at 90° to it.
  • The normal force is a reaction: the harder you press on a surface, the harder it pushes back.
  • Examples: the floor under your feet, a chair under you, the ground under a car.

Friction

Friction is a force that opposes (works against) motion when two surfaces rub or try to slide past each other.

Up close, surfaces are bumpy

Even smooth surfaces have tiny bumps that catch on each other. Rougher surface means more friction.

Three kinds of friction

TypeWhat happens
StaticObject is not moving yet, like a heavy sofa that won't budge
SlidingObject slides across a surface, like a sled on snow
RollingWheel or ball rolls, which is much weaker than sliding

Useful friction

  • Shoe soles gripping the ground
  • Brakes stopping a bike
  • Rubbing hands to make heat
  • Matches lighting

Unwanted friction

  • Machine parts wearing out
  • Heat lost in engines
  • Made smaller with oil, wheels, smooth surfaces

Magnetism

Magnetism is a non-contact force. A magnet can attract (pull) or repel (push) without touching.
The magnetic field: lines leave the North pole and curve back into the South pole. It is strongest at the poles.

Attracted to magnets

Iron, steel, nickel, cobalt: nails, paper clips, steel cans, fridge doors.

Not attracted

Wood, plastic, glass, paper, rubber, aluminium foil, copper coins.

A 5 kg box rests on the floor (its weight is about 50 N). Choose a surface, then slide the applied force. Watch what friction does.

Balanced forces

Equal forces in opposite directions. Net force is 0 N, so a still object stays still and a moving one keeps going at the same speed.

Unbalanced forces

One force is bigger. Net force is more than 0 N, so the object speeds up, slows down or changes direction.

Worked examples (filled in for you)

1. A force is a push or a pull, measured in newtons.

2. A girl kicks a ball. The kick is an applied force, and it is a contact force because her foot touches the ball.

3. A cup sits on a table. Gravity pulls it down, and the table pushes it up with the normal force. These forces are balanced.

4. A sled slides across snow and slows down. The force that slows it is friction, and it acts in the opposite direction to the motion.

5. A magnet picks up a paper clip across a small gap. This is a non-contact force called magnetism.

6. Two magnets with the same poles facing each other repel. Opposite poles attract.

7. A box has a 30 N applied force to the right and 12 N of friction to the left. Net force = 30 − 12 = 18 N to the right. The forces are unbalanced.
