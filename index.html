Standard (a)(1)(A)

What Is Scientific Inquiry?

Big ideaScientific inquiry is the planned and deliberate investigation of the natural world, using scientific and engineering practices.

That sentence has a lot of big words, so let's take it apart piece by piece.

Inquiry
Asking questions and searching for answers.
Planned
You decide the steps before you start. You do not just poke around.
Deliberate
You do it on purpose, with a goal, not by accident.
Natural world
Everything in nature: weather, plants, animals, rocks, your body, light, forces.
Phenomenon
Something you can observe happening, like a rainbow or a ball rolling to a stop.
Practices
The things scientists and engineers actually do: ask, plan, test, measure, explain, design.

The inquiry loop

Inquiry is not a straight line. It is a loop, because every answer usually leads to a brand-new question.

  1. Observe
  2. Ask a question
  3. Plan
  4. Investigate
  5. Explain
  6. New question
Filled in for you

Complete the sentence

Scientific inquiry is the planned and deliberate investigation of the natural world using scientific and engineering practices.

Is each of these scientific inquiry? Yes if it is planned, on purpose, and about nature.

  • Measuring how fast ice melts in sun and in shade: Yes. It is planned and about nature.
  • Rolling a ball down a ramp and recording how far it goes: Yes. It is planned, with measurements.
  • Guessing the answer and never checking it: No. There is no plan and no evidence.

The four methods of investigation

The method scientists use depends on the question they are asking. The four methods are descriptive, correlative, comparative and experimental. You will meet each one in Lesson 2. The right method should match both the question and your grade level.

RememberInquiry is planned, deliberate, and about the natural world. Evidence is what makes it science.

Four Ways to Investigate

Big ideaDifferent questions need different kinds of investigation. Two quick questions tell you which one to use: Do I have a hypothesis? and Will I compare a treatment with a control?
MethodHypothesis?What you doExample
DescriptiveNoCollect data and record observations. You do not compare.Record the weather in your town every day for two weeks.
CorrelativeYesMeasure two things as they naturally happen and look for a relationship.Do taller students have longer feet?
ComparativeYesMeasure variables, change a condition between setups, and compare the results.Which of three paper towel brands soaks up the most water?
ExperimentalYesTest the hypothesis by comparing a treatment group with a control group.Does fertilizer make bean plants grow taller than plants with no fertilizer?

Which one do I use?

  1. Do I have a hypothesis?
  2. No → Descriptive
  1. Do I have a hypothesis?
  2. Yes: treatment vs control? Yes → Experimental
  1. Do I have a hypothesis?
  2. Yes: treatment vs control? No → Correlative or Comparative
Filled in for you

Sort these four investigations

What happenedTypeWhy
Mia records the temperature, wind and clouds at noon every day for 14 days.DescriptiveShe only collects observations. There is no hypothesis and no comparison.
Ken predicts that taller classmates have bigger feet. He measures the height and foot length of 20 classmates.CorrelativeHis hypothesis predicts a relationship between two measured things.
Ava soaks up water with 3 brands of paper towel and compares how much each holds.ComparativeShe compares results across conditions. No group is left untreated.
Leo thinks salt makes ice melt faster. He salts 5 ice cubes and leaves 5 ice cubes plain.ExperimentalHe tests a hypothesis by comparing a treatment (salt) with a control (no salt).
RememberDifferent books sometimes use slightly different names. Do not worry about that. Ask the two questions: hypothesis? and treatment vs control?

Scientists and Engineers

Big ideaScientists ask why and explain the world. Engineers ask how and design solutions to problems. Both use tools and models.

Scientific practices

Students ask questions, plan and conduct investigations to answer them, and explain phenomena using appropriate tools and models.

Engineering practices

Students identify problems and design solutions using appropriate tools and models.

Two loops

The science loop:

  1. Observe
  2. Ask a question
  3. Plan and investigate
  4. Collect data
  5. Explain

The engineering loop:

  1. Define the problem
  2. Imagine and design
  3. Build a model
  4. Test
  5. Improve
Filled in for you

Same topic, two different jobs: paper airplanes

ScientistEngineer
Starts witha questiona problem
AsksDoes the length of the wings change how far a paper airplane flies?Can we design a paper airplane that flies at least 5 m and carries one paperclip?
DoesFlies planes with different wing lengths and measures the distances.Builds a model, tests it, and improves it until it works.
Ends withan explanation supported by dataa solution that meets the requirements

Criteria are what the solution must do: fly 5 m or more and carry one paperclip. Constraints are the limits: one sheet of paper, 20 minutes and 10 cm of tape.

RememberFor at least 40% of your class time you should be doing real science: asking questions, finding problems, and safely carrying out investigations in the classroom, lab or outdoors. Science is something you do.

Standard (b)(1)(A)

Asking Questions and Defining Problems

Big ideaScience starts with a testable question. Engineering starts with a clearly defined problem. Both begin with something you notice.

Where do questions come from?

Good questions can come from observations or information in a text, a phenomenon, a model, or an earlier investigation. Any time you think “huh, why did that happen?”, you have found a question.

What makes a question testable?

A testable question can be answered by measuring or observing. A handy pattern: “How does ___ affect ___?”

Filled in for you

Testable or not?

QuestionTestable?Why
How does the amount of sunlight change how tall a bean plant grows?YesYou can give plants different light and measure their height.
Which flower colour is the prettiest?NoIt is an opinion. You cannot measure “pretty”.
Does the temperature of water change how fast sugar dissolves?YesYou can measure the water temperature and the time to dissolve.
Should people be allowed to keep pet snakes?NoIt depends on values and opinions. That is a social or ethical decision, not a science test.
Filled in for you

From observation to question

Observation: Ice cubes in a cup in the sun melted faster than ice cubes in a cup in the shade.

I wonder: Is it the sunlight that makes the ice melt faster?

Testable question: How does sunlight affect how fast ice melts?

Defining an engineering problem

A well-defined problem says what is wrong, who it affects, what the solution must do (criteria) and what limits you have (constraints).

Filled in for you

The flooding school garden

Problem: After heavy rain the school garden floods and the seedlings drown.

Who is affected: the students and teachers who grow vegetables there

Criteria (must do): drain the water within 1 hour and keep seedlings in place

Constraints (limits): a budget of 50 dollars, built by students in 2 weeks, recycled materials

RememberTestable = you can measure it. Opinion questions are not testable by science.

Standard (b)(1)(B)

Planning a Fair Test

Big ideaIn a fair test you change only one thing, measure its effect, and keep everything else the same.
Independent variable
The one thing you change on purpose. (Fertilizer or no fertilizer.)
Dependent variable
What you measure to see the effect. It depends on the change. (Plant height.)
Controlled variables
Everything you keep the same so the test is fair. (Soil, light, water, pot size, bean type.)
Control group
The group that gets no treatment. You compare against it.
Treatment group
The group that gets the thing you are testing.
Hypothesis
A tentative, testable prediction, written as “If… then… because…”.
Filled in for you

A complete investigation plan

Part of the planFilled in
QuestionHow does fertilizer affect the height of bean plants?
HypothesisIf bean plants are given fertilizer, then they will grow taller than plants without it, because fertilizer adds nutrients that help plants grow.
Independent variableFertilizer (with or without)
Dependent variablePlant height in centimetres after 14 days
Controlled variablesSame type of bean, same soil, same pot size, same amount of water, same sunlight, same temperature
Control group3 pots with no fertilizer
Treatment group3 pots with fertilizer
Trials3 plants in each group, so we can find a mean
SafetyWear gloves with fertilizer, do not touch your face, wash hands afterwards

Why change only one thing?

If one group got fertilizer and more sunlight, and it grew taller, you could not tell whether the fertilizer or the sunlight did it. Changing one variable at a time is what makes a test fair.

Filled in for you

Spot the unfair test

Mia puts some bean pots on a sunny window with 100 mL of water and others in a dark cupboard with 50 mL of water. Is this a fair test?

No. She changed two variables (light and water). To make it fair she should change only one, for example the light, and give every pot the same amount of water.

RememberHypothesis recipe: If (the change), then (what will happen), because (the science reason).

Standard (b)(1)(C)

Lab Safety

Big ideaSafety comes before science. Good scientists plan for safety every single time, in the classroom, the lab and outdoors.

Your class follows safety standards approved for schools. They all come down to common sense, planning ahead, and listening to your teacher.

Always

  • Read all the steps and listen to instructions before you start.
  • Wear safety goggles when told to.
  • Tie back long hair and keep loose sleeves out of the way.
  • Wear closed-toe shoes.
  • Tell your teacher at once about spills, breakages and injuries.
  • Treat a hot plate as hot, and use heat-protective gloves or tongs.
  • Wash your hands and clean up when you finish.

Never

  • Eat, drink or chew gum in the lab.
  • Taste chemicals or lab materials.
  • Sniff a substance directly. If told to smell it, gently waft the fumes toward you with your hand.
  • Run or play around.
  • Pick up broken glass with bare hands.
  • Mix materials that are not in the instructions.
  • Leave a hot plate on when you are not using it.

Safety outdoors (field investigations)

  • Stay with your group and your teacher.
  • Wear sun protection and sensible shoes, and carry water.
  • Do not touch or taste unknown plants, animals or mushrooms.
  • Watch your footing near water, slopes and roads.
Filled in for you

What should you do?

SituationWhat to do
A chemical splashes into your eye.Go to the eyewash station straight away and tell your teacher.
A glass beaker breaks.Stop, keep others away, and tell your teacher. Do not pick up the glass with bare hands.
You need to smell a liquid.Waft the fumes gently toward your nose with your hand. Never put your nose to the container.
You have long hair and are using a hot plate.Tie your hair back and use heat-protective gloves.
RememberIf you are not sure whether something is safe, stop and ask your teacher first.

Science Tools

Big ideaChoosing the right tool and using it correctly is how scientists get measurements they can trust.

Measuring tools

Graduated cylindervolume of liquid · mL

Read it at eye level, at the bottom of the curved water surface (the meniscus).

Metric rulerlength · cm, mm

Line the object up with the 0 mark and look straight down at the scale.

Balancemass · g, kg

Set it to zero first. Put the object in the middle of the pan.

Scaleweight or mass of bigger objects

Make sure it sits on a flat surface and reads zero before you start.

Thermometer / temperature probetemperature · °C

Wait until the reading stops changing. Never use it to stir.

Timing devicetime · s

A stopwatch or timer. Start and stop on a clear signal, and repeat the timing.

Spring scale / force sensorforce · N (newtons)

Hold it straight up and down and read it when it stops moving.

Tools for looking closer and testing

Hand lens

Magnifies small details of rocks, insects, leaves.

Microscope and slides

Shows cells and other things too small to see with your eyes.

Petri dish

A shallow, covered dish for growing or observing tiny organisms.

Dissecting kit

Used carefully, with your teacher, to examine specimens.

Magnet

Tests which materials are magnetic and shows magnetic forces.

pH indicator

Changes colour to show whether a liquid is acidic, neutral or basic.

Hot plate

Heats materials. Always assume it is hot and wear heat-protective gloves.

Laboratory ware

Beakers, flasks, test tubes and similar containers for holding, mixing and heating.

Periodic table

Lists the elements, so you can look up their type and properties.

Models and life science models

Stand-ins for things that are too big, too small or too hard to study directly.

Satellite images

Show Earth from space: weather, land, oceans and ice.

Tools that model wave behaviour

A slinky or rope shows how waves move and carry energy.

Lab notebook or journal

Where you record plans, observations, data and ideas as they happen.

Collecting Data with SI Units

Big ideaScientists all over the world measure with the same units, the International System of Units (SI), so everyone can understand and compare results.

Data are the pieces of information you collect as evidence. There are two kinds. Quantitative data are measurements with numbers and units. Qualitative data describe what you notice with your senses.

SI units to know

Prefixes make units bigger or smaller

Standard (b)(1)(F)

Tables, Graphs and Repeated Trials

Big ideaRepeat your test several times and find the mean. Then organise the results in tables, graphs, maps and charts so the pattern is easy to see.

Why repeat trials?

One measurement can be off because of a small slip: a shaky hand, a late stopwatch click. When you repeat the test (each repeat is a trial) and take the mean (the average), those little mistakes even out and your result is more reliable.

Mean = (add up all the values) ÷ (how many values there are).

A good graph has

  • A title that says what it shows.
  • Labelled axes with units, like “Time (days)” and “Height (cm)”.
  • The independent variable on the horizontal (x) axis and the dependent variable on the vertical (y) axis.

Standard (b)(1)(G) · (b)(2)(A)

Models

Big ideaA model is a stand-in for something real. Every model helps in some ways and has limits in others.

Scientists and engineers develop and use models to represent phenomena (things that happen), systems (parts working together), processes (steps over time) and solutions to engineering problems.

Why use a model?

Too big

The solar system, or the whole Earth.

Too small

Atoms and cells.

Too slow or too fast

The rock cycle takes millions of years. A chemical reaction can take a split second.

Too dangerous or costly

A volcano, or a real bridge you have not tested yet.

Advantages and limitations

When you judge a model, ask about its size, scale, properties and materials. How is it different from the real thing?

Filled in for you

Judging four models

ModelAdvantage (what it shows well)Limitation (how it differs)
GlobeShows the shapes and positions of continents and oceans.Far smaller than Earth, made of plastic, and cannot show weather or moving plates.
Clay model of a cellShows the parts of a cell and where they sit.Much bigger than a real cell, not alive, and clay is not what cells are made of.
Popsicle-stick bridgeLets engineers test a design before building the real one.Wood sticks are not steel, and a small bridge carries weight differently from a huge one.
Water cycle diagramShows the order of the steps.It is flat and still. It does not show amounts or real time.
RememberNo model is perfect. A good scientist uses a model for what it does well, and always names its limits.

Standard (a)(3) · (b)(1)(H)

Hypothesis, Theory and Law

Big ideaScientists use five different words on purpose: observation, inference, hypothesis, theory and law. Each one has its own job.
Observation
Using your senses (or tools) to gather information from a primary source. It can be qualitative (descriptions) or quantitative (numbers).
Inference
A conclusion you reach from observations plus reasoning that is supported by evidence.
Hypothesis
A tentative, testable statement. Evidence can support it or not support it.
Theory
A well-established, highly reliable explanation of natural phenomena. It can be tested by many independent researchers, and it may change when new evidence or technology arrives.
Law
A statement that describes a pattern in nature, often as a mathematical relationship. It tells what happens, not why.
Filled in for you

Name that statement

StatementWhich one?Why
The puddle was 30 cm wide at 8 a.m. and gone by 2 p.m.ObservationIt reports what was measured and seen.
The puddle disappeared because the water evaporated.InferenceIt is a conclusion based on the observation plus reasoning.
I think plants near the window will grow taller because they get more light.HypothesisIt is tentative and can be tested.
All living things are made of cells, and cells come from pre-existing cells.Theory (cell theory)It is a well-tested explanation backed by huge amounts of evidence.
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another.LawIt describes what always happens.

Myth

“A theory is just a guess.”

Fact

The guess is the hypothesis. A theory is a strong explanation supported by a great deal of evidence.

Not every question belongs to science

Science works with questions it can test. “Which music is best?” cannot be tested, so it is outside the realm of science. Science can give evidence about a decision, such as how a new factory affects the air. Whether to build it also depends on values, and that is an ethical and social decision people make together.

RememberHypothesis = testable idea. Theory = well-tested explanation (why). Law = describes a pattern (what).

Standard (b)(2)(A)–(D)

Analyzing and Interpreting Data

Big ideaData only become evidence when you look for patterns, do the maths, hunt for errors and judge the design.

Find the pattern

Look at your table or graph. As one thing goes up, does the other go up too? Go down? Show no pattern at all? A pattern that links two things is a relationship or correlation. A correlation shows the two things move together. It does not by itself prove that one causes the other.

Describe the data with numbers

Filled in for you

Plant heights (cm): 10, 12, 12, 13, 18

MeasureHow to find itAnswer
Meanadd them up, divide by how many(10+12+12+13+18) ÷ 5 = 65 ÷ 5 = 13 cm
Medianput them in order, take the middle one10, 12, 12, 13, 18, so 12 cm
Modethe value that appears most often12 cm (it appears twice)
Rangebiggest minus smallest18 − 10 = 8 cm

Use maths to compare

Control mean = 10 cm. Treatment mean = 13 cm. The difference is 13 − 10 = 3 cm. As a percentage of the control: 3 ÷ 10 = 0.30, so the treated plants were 30% taller.

Sources of error

Every investigation has some error. Good scientists look for it and say so.

Source of errorExampleHow to reduce it
MeasuringReading the ruler from an angle.Read at eye level and repeat the measurement.
EquipmentA balance that was not set to zero.Check and zero tools first.
Uncontrolled variableOne pot got extra sunshine.Keep every other condition the same.
Too few trialsOnly one plant per group.Use more trials and find the mean.
RecordingWriting 31 instead of 13.Record straight away and double-check.

Limitations are different from errors. They are things your investigation cannot tell you. Our fertilizer test used only bean plants for only 14 days, so it cannot tell us about every plant or about a whole growing season.

Evaluate the design

For an experiment ask: Was it a fair test with one changed variable? Was there a control? Were there enough trials? For an engineering design ask: Did it meet the criteria and stay within the constraints? What should we improve?

Filled in for you

Evaluating the paper airplane

Distances flown: 4.0 m, 4.5 m and 4.1 m. Mean = (4.0 + 4.5 + 4.1) ÷ 3 = 12.6 ÷ 3 = 4.2 m.

Criterion 1: fly at least 5 m. Not met, because 4.2 m is less than 5 m.

Criterion 2: carry one paperclip. Met.

Next step: change one thing, such as the wing size, and test again with three trials.

RememberMean, median, mode, range. Look for patterns, outliers and errors. A correlation is not the same as a cause.

Standard (b)(3)(A)–(C)

Explaining and Arguing with Evidence

Big ideaA good explanation has a claim, backed by evidence and linked with reasoning. Then you share it clearly and discuss it respectfully.

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Claim
Your answer to the question, in one sentence.
Evidence
The data and observations that support it. Use numbers and units.
Reasoning
The science idea that explains why the evidence supports the claim.
Filled in for you

A complete explanation

Question: How does fertilizer affect bean plant height?

Claim: Fertilizer makes bean plants grow taller.

Evidence: After 14 days the plants with fertilizer had a mean height of 13 cm and the plants without fertilizer had a mean height of 10 cm. Each group had 3 plants.

Reasoning: Plants use nutrients to build new growth. Fertilizer adds nutrients, so the plants with fertilizer could grow more.

The same idea for an engineering solution

Claim: Design B should be chosen. Evidence: It flew 5.2 m, 5.6 m and 6.0 m, a mean of 5.6 m, which beats the 5 m criterion every time. Reasoning: Its longer wings give it more lift, so it stays up longer.

Communicating your findings

You can share explanations and solutions on your own or in a group, in many formats: a written report, a labelled graph, a poster, a model, a talk, or a lab notebook page. Choose the format that makes your evidence clearest to your audience.

Respectful scientific argument

Scientists disagree all the time. The goal is to find out what the evidence says, not to win.

Say things like

  • “What evidence did you use?”
  • “I see your point, but my data show something different. Can we compare?”
  • “I changed my mind because the evidence showed…”

Avoid

  • Making it personal or putting people down.
  • Saying “I just know” with no evidence.
  • Ignoring data that does not fit your idea.
RememberDisagree with the idea, never the person. Let the evidence decide.

Standard (b)(4)(A)–(C)

Scientists, Society and STEM Careers

Big ideaScience changes the world, so we weigh costs and benefits, check where information comes from, and learn from many different scientists.

Research changes society

Past and present research shapes what people know and how people live. Studying germs led to vaccines and clean water systems. Satellites help forecast storms. When you study the process of science and the contributions of many different scientists, you see that anyone can add to what we know.

Cost-benefit analysis

A cost-benefit analysis lists what a decision costs and what it gains, then compares them.

Filled in for you

Should our town build a solar farm?

BenefitsCosts
Clean electricity with no smoke
Less air pollution
Lower energy bills over many years		Money to build it
Land that cannot be used for other things
Needs a back-up for cloudy days and nights

Science provides the evidence (how much power, how much pollution). People then weigh the costs and benefits and decide, using their values too.

Judging information sources

Before you trust information, check four things:

Credibility

Who wrote it? Do they have expertise? Is the organisation reliable?

Accuracy

Do other trustworthy sources agree? Is it up to date? Does it show evidence?

Cost-effectiveness

Is the benefit worth the money and effort it takes?

Methods

How was it tested? Was it a fair test, with a control and enough trials?

Scientists who changed things

Ibn al-Haytham

c. 965–1040

Studied light and vision and tested his ideas with experiments, which is why he is often called a pioneer of the experimental method.

Marie Curie

1867–1934

Researched radioactivity. She won Nobel Prizes in two different sciences: Physics (1903) and Chemistry (1911).

Katherine Johnson

1918–2020

A NASA mathematician whose calculations helped early American crewed space flights, including John Glenn’s orbit in 1962.

Mae Jemison

born 1956

A doctor and engineer who became the first African American woman in space, in 1992.

Wangari Maathai

1940–2011

A Kenyan environmental scientist who founded the Green Belt Movement, which planted millions of trees. She received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004.

Exploring STEM careers

You can find out about careers in science, technology, engineering and mathematics from many places:

  • Museums and libraries for exhibits, books and events.
  • Professional organisations that describe the work and how to train for it.
  • Private companies that share what their teams do.
  • Online platforms with videos, courses and career guides (check the source, as you learned above).
  • Mentors who work in a STEM field. Ask them what a normal day looks like and what they studied.
RememberWeigh costs and benefits. Check credibility, accuracy and methods. Many people from many places have added to science, and you can too.
